# Which second line behaves like Tanko? — 17 September 2026

Wei Ming's question, and it is the right one: forget margin for a moment and ask
**which candidate can I actually afford to keep on a shelf.** A line you can stock
is a line you can win exclusivity on. A line you cannot stock leaves you a trader
forever.

Scored on the four things that decide that: **capital per unit, stock turn, SKU
matrix, floor space.**

---

## 1. The benchmark — why the Tanko line works

| | Tanko |
|---|---|
| Capital per unit | Low to moderate |
| Stock turn | **Continuous** — every factory buys benches, cabinets, lockers |
| SKU matrix | Wide, but each unit is cheap, so breadth is affordable |
| Floor space | Flat-packable and stackable |
| Comes back for repair? | **Never** |

That combination is why you can hold 1,517 products without the inventory
strangling you. Any second line should be measured against this row, not against
a margin percentage.

## 3. #1 — Casters, and why they beat everything else

Casters are the only candidate that scores Tanko-like on **all four** inventory
criteria *and* sells to the customer you already have *and* has a verified open
territory.

- **They are wear items.** A castor fails, gets replaced, and the buyer does not
  shop around for a RM30 part — they buy from whoever has it. That is the highest
  stock turn of anything on this list.
- **Every Tanko trolley, mobile bench, workstation and mould rack you have ever
  installed rolls on them.** Your installed base *is* the market. You already know
  who those customers are and you have been to their sites.
- **A complete range fits on one rack.** No new warehouse, no new floor space.
- **Nothing ever comes back for repair.**
- **JEIN YI (1981, ~50 staff) exports only to the US, Japan, Germany, UK and
  Canada — no Asian exposure at all.** That is the cleanest open-territory signal
  in this whole search. **HENG TA** is similar: Americas, Singapore and Spain only.
- **HO CASTER (1990) has the depth — 269 products** including 2-tonne PU heavy
  duty, elastic rubber with stoppers and nylon trolley wheels — **but already
  reaches Malaysia.** Use them as the benchmark for what a complete line contains,
  and ask JEIN YI whether they can match it.

**Watch:** Tanko's own trolleys ship with castors fitted. Replacement and upgrade
castors is a genuinely different market from supplying Tanko's components, but
mention it to Tanko rather than letting them find out.

**Two things in their own sales pitch are aimed straight at your problem:**

1. **"Unlimited MOQ, provide free samples."** No minimum order. That removes the
   single biggest objection to a low-capital start.
2. **3D files downloadable from their site.** That is how components get sold to
   design engineers — they drop your part into their CAD model and it is specified
   before anyone asks the price.

**The buyer exists here.** Malaysia has a substantial enclosure and switchgear
fabrication industry — TAMCO, Elecol Switchgear, Vblue Electrical, Grand Spektra,
CVS Enclosure — and every panel they build needs locks, hinges, handles and
gaskets. **No Malaysian Tai Sam distributor surfaced in any search.**

**The catch, and it is the real one: this is a different customer.** Tai Sam sells
to *people who build cabinets*, not to *people who buy cabinets*. Your website
ranks for storage terms, your customer list is factories buying benches, and your
crew installs finished goods. Selling panel locks to a switchboard fabricator is a
component-supply business: scheduled repeat orders, price-sensitive, and it *needs*
shelf stock to win — which is exactly why the inventory economics work so well.

**It is the best stocking profile here. It is also the biggest change of business.**

## 5. What this says about Chung Fu

Nothing that kills it — but it reframes it. **Chung Fu is not a stocking line and
never will be.** High capital, low turn, bulky, a wide and expensive matrix. That
is fine, as long as you treat it as what it is:

> **An opportunistic high-margin line, sold back-to-back, with one demonstration
> unit — not the line you build the next twenty years on.**

Send the Chung Fu email anyway. The margin is real and the territory is verified
open. Just do not expect it to become a Tanko-shaped business, and do not let it
be your only second line.

**The pairing that actually makes sense:** one stocking line that turns (casters or
Tai Sam) plus one opportunistic margin line that does not (Chung Fu). They fund
different things. The stocking line pays the overhead; the capital-equipment line
pays for the year.

1. **Email JEIN YI and HENG TA about castors.** Best all-round fit: open
   territory, your existing customer, trivial inventory cost, nothing comes back.
   Ask HO CASTER too — they already reach Malaysia, but their 269-product range
   tells you what a complete line should contain.
2. **Email TAI SAM.** Ask two questions first: *is Malaysia open*, and *who
   currently buys your parts in Malaysia*. Their export markets are not published,
   so that is the unknown to resolve before spending more thought on it.
3. **Send the Chung Fu email as drafted** — back-to-back, one demo unit, no
   exclusivity ask yet.
4. **Leave Good Hand.** Right product shape, but Asia is covered and they run their
   own US and UK branches.
5. **Leave air tools** until you have decided about a repair bench.
